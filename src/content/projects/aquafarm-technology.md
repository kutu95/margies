Margaret River Aquafarm is a working aquaponics greenhouse in Forest Grove, near Margaret River. Fish and plants share a recirculating system. The only routine input is fish food. There are no chemical pesticides, and there is no useful version of “we’ll check it when we remember”.

I operate the farm. I also built the technology that watches it. Those are not separate careers. A greenhouse that fails on a hot afternoon does not care that the person who designed the sensor is also the person who has to harvest lettuce on Saturday.

## What needs watching

Water quality, temperature, pumps, and the ordinary environmental facts of a plastic greenhouse in the south-west: heat, humidity, the difference between a still morning and a wind that finds a tear in the plastic. Some of that can be felt by walking in. Some of it cannot. pH and dissolved waste do not announce themselves politely.

The systems are a mix of Arduino sensor nodes, Raspberry Pi collectors, wireless links, and custom web and mobile software. They record history, draw dashboards, and send alerts when a reading leaves the range that the plants and fish will tolerate.

## Not a product, a place

There are commercial farm platforms. Most of them assume a different kind of farm, a different budget, or a willingness to rent someone else’s cloud for the privilege of knowing whether a pump is still on. I needed something that matched this greenhouse, these sensors, and the way I actually look at data — including on a phone while standing in the aisle.

Automation exists where it is safer than a person remembering. Alerts exist where a person still has to act. I am not interested in a greenhouse that believes it can run itself. I am interested in one that tells the truth early.

## Why it is in the portfolio

This is the clearest example I have of software plus electronics plus real infrastructure. The farm is not a demo. If the dashboard is wrong, fish die. That is a better test than a staging environment.

It is also a reminder that “Internet of Things” is a marketing phrase and “a node in a damp greenhouse that still reports in two years” is an engineering problem.
